The page beneath the calculations

Every textbook question here is asking for "some vector." Turned around, every one of them is really solving an equation system.
Question numbers and pages refer to Math AA HL, pp. 272–322.

One line
Count how many equations you're given, and you already know how many degrees of freedom the answer has.
Whether a question asks for "any two" or "exactly two" isn't the setter's taste — it's the dimension of the solution space deciding for them.

Three ways to read it

One · count the equations, and you know the degrees of freedom

One equation → a plane (infinitely many solutions, not all parallel to each other); two equations → a line (everything written as $k\cdot$ some vector); add a length condition and you get exactly two points, front and back.
This is why 12L Q17 asks for "any two" while 12N.1 Q11's answer is stubbornly "two" — not a coincidence, the dimension decided it.

Two · rank collapse means parallel

For a $2\times3$ matrix, the two rows being proportional $\iff\mathbf a\parallel\mathbf b \iff\mathbf a\times\mathbf b=\mathbf 0\iff$ the solution space grows from 1D to 2D.
"Parallel," "collinear," "cross product is zero," "area is zero" show up in four different sections of the textbook. Underneath, they're the same fact.

Three · rank + nullity = 3, always

Treat $T(\mathbf x)=\mathbf a\times\mathbf x$ as a map: nullity = 1 (the null space is $\mathrm{span}\{\mathbf a\}$), rank = 2 (the image is $\mathbf a^\perp$).
"$\mathbf a\times\mathbf b$ is always perpendicular to $\mathbf a$" is just another way of saying the image is $\mathbf a^\perp$.

So what do you actually write in the exam

Exactly what you'd write anyway. $k(3,-1,1)$, $\pm(\dots)$, the right parameter carried through, the right number of answers given — that's what the structure looks like on a mark scheme. None of the words above need to appear on the page.

Knowing the structure isn't about writing it differently. It's knowing how many answers there should be before you've solved anything.
