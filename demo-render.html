Rendering a Triangle

Every frame in a game is these two steps repeated: the cross product decides which pixels get covered, the dot product decides how bright they are. Colours follow the previous demo — violet is the cross product, amber is the dot product.

The cross product does two jobs in the pipeline

First, it gives the normal n = (B−A)×(C−A), handed off to lighting. Second, it gives the signed area: its sign tells you whether the triangle faces the camera or away from it (back faces get thrown out immediately, cutting the drawing workload roughly in half), and its magnitude is used to compute barycentric coordinates — the three small triangles formed by the pixel point and the three edges, whose area ratios are the interpolation weights that vertex colours, texture coordinates, and depth all ride on.

Edge functions: the heart of rasterisation

Deciding whether pixel P is inside the triangle needs no system of inequalities — just the z-component of three cross products, (B−A)×(P−A). All three the same sign means inside; mixed signs means outside. These few multiplications and subtractions run in parallel across millions of pixels — exactly what the GPU is doing, every frame.

The dot product gives shading: a projection again

Lambert's cosine law, brightness ∝ max(0, N·L): the more squarely a surface faces the light, the brighter it is; tilt it and it dims; turn it away and it goes to zero. It's the same formula as the electric flux Φ = E·A from the last demo — both are asking "how much flux lands per unit area." Rotate the triangle and watch: the instant the normal passes 90°, brightness drops straight to zero.
