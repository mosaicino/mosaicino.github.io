A shadow is a ray-plane intersection

A rooftop corner P, walking along −L away from the sun, hits the ground at exactly its shadow point. The ground's normal is n = (0,1,0), the intersection parameter is t = (P·n)/(L·n), and the landing point is P′ = P − tL. That denominator is exactly a dot product — the lower the sun, the closer L·n gets to zero, and the longer the shadow, tending to infinity right at sunrise and sunset.

Wall shading is still N·L

Four walls plus a roof, each with its own normal. Which face is bright and which is dark just comes down to max(0, N·L) — the same formula as the Lambert shading in the last demo, and the electric flux in the one before that. Turn to noon and the roof is brightest; in the morning the east wall is lit and the west is dark, and it flips exactly in the evening.

Shadow area relies on the cross product

The shadow is an irregular polygon with no formula to reach for. But take the vertices in order, cross each consecutive pair, and sum: S = ½|Σ Pᵢ × Pᵢ₊₁|, and the area falls right out — this is the shoelace formula used in surveying and GIS. At bottom it's splitting the polygon into a fan of triangles rooted at the origin; each triangle's signed area is exactly a cross product, and the signs cancel until what's left is the true area.

Sundials: why the gnomon sits at an angle

Tick "Add a sundial." The gnomon's direction is (0, sin φ, −cos φ) — exactly parallel to the Earth's axis, pointing at the celestial pole. Only when it's parallel to the axis does the shadow sweep at a constant rate around a fixed axis, making the hour lines' angles independent of the season: drag the date from winter to summer solstice and that ring of hour marks doesn't budge, only the shadow's length changes. Tilt the gnomon off-axis and the sundial keeps bad time across the year. Worth noting: what it reads is true solar time, which can differ from a clock by up to sixteen minutes — that gap is the equation of time.

What this is actually good for

Residential sunlight codes require continuous full-window sun on ground-floor sills on the winter solstice or the coldest day of the year. Set the latitude to your city and drag the date to the winter solstice — the envelope in the right-hand plot is exactly the area this building shadows across the whole day. Required spacing between buildings gets measured straight off a plot like this one.
