The cross product builds the coordinate frame

The "east, north, zenith" under the station's feet doesn't fall out of the sky — it has to be built on the spot. The zenith direction Û is just the station's position vector normalised; east comes from Û × ŷ (ŷ is the Earth's axis), and north is filled in by Ê × Û. Three mutually perpendicular unit vectors, assembled with two cross products — the same trick as building a camera basis with right = forward × up in a game engine. Near the poles Û becomes nearly parallel to the Earth's axis, the cross product shrinks toward zero, and east loses its definition — which is why polar stations need a different coordinate scheme.

The dot product measures the angles

Once you have the line-of-sight vector v = P − S, elevation is the complement of its angle to the zenith direction: sin El = v·Û / |v|. Azimuth comes from projecting v onto the horizontal plane and reading off how much of it lands along east versus north: Az = atan2(v·Ê, v·N̂). Visibility is even simpler — v·Û > 0 means above the horizon, less than zero means blocked by the Earth. Three dot products, and both antenna pointing angles are settled.

Worth a side note: the textbook formula tan Az = tan Δλ / sin φ needs a quadrant correction split into four cases depending on hemisphere and whether the satellite is east or west — get one wrong and you're off by 180°. The vector method's atan2 reads the quadrant straight off the signs of the two projected components, one line, works the same in either hemisphere — drag latitude across the equator and the reading stays continuous.

Why only one altitude stays geostationary

Drag the altitude slider and watch the orbital period. By Kepler's third law T = 2π√(r³/μ), period depends only on orbital radius. For a satellite to look pinned in the sky, its period has to equal one sidereal day, 23h56m04s, which works backward to a unique r = 42164 km — altitude 35786 km. Go a little higher and the period lengthens, so the satellite drifts west relative to the ground; go lower and it drifts east — that's exactly how the daily-drift reading is computed, and the sub-satellite longitude creeps day by day.

What these numbers are actually for

Elevation decides how high to tilt the antenna, and also how much atmosphere the signal has to punch through: below roughly 5–10° elevation, links usually stop working — rain fade and ground clutter both get much worse. Drag latitude up to 81° and you'll find a geostationary satellite simply never clears the horizon — exactly why high-latitude regions switch to polar or highly elliptical orbits instead. Slant range divided by the speed of light gives the one-way delay: about 120 ms for geostationary, and the round trip plus relay processing adds up to nearly half a second — the source of that noticeable pause on a satellite phone call.
